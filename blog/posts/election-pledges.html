Tracking and Forecasting Pledges in Indian Election Manifestos

Two manifestos from the same party might both promise a subsidy. One might offer it to all households, while a later edition restricts it to a smaller group. Similar wording can make the earlier pledge easy to find. Understanding the change requires noticing exactly which words alter the commitment.

I built this project around that gap. It uses the passage table from the companion sentiment study and separates four tasks: finding candidate pledges, retrieving earlier counterparts, classifying the relationship between statements, and forecasting the mix of policy topics. Each step has its own evaluation. A high score at one stage leaves open what happens at the others.

Giving Each Pledge a Source#

The source corpus consists of 21 English Lok Sabha manifestos from six parties and four elections between 2009 and 2024. The two projects used byte-identical copies of the 9,572-passage source table. The pledge project split it into 15,376 candidate statements. Each statement retains a parent passage identifier, document identifier, PDF page, and character offsets. Those fields let a reader inspect a proposed revision in the original paragraph, where qualifiers and references can affect what the party intended.

The statement boundaries create their own judgement calls. A bullet might say “expand this scheme to all districts” and leave the scheme name in a preceding heading. That segment could be hard to retrieve by name and ambiguous as a standalone commitment. The link to the source passage gives a reviewer the context to check the interpretation. Of the 848 OCR passages, 840 came from just four documents. That concentration matters if a proposed revision depends on an amount or date, since a character error in one edition can look like a material edit. The original PDF remains the place to check it. Finding Pledges with Labels That Can Abstain#

The detector uses rules to label clear commitments and clear non-pledges. If the rules cannot decide, the statement stays unlabelled. A logistic regression then learns from historical rule-labelled statements, using TF-IDF features and frozen MiniLM embeddings. This produces scores for statements outside the rules’ coverage and gives the downstream stages a threshold they can use to narrow the candidate pool.

The detector trained on 1,906 rule-labelled statements from elections through 2019. Of 4,020 statements in the 2024 set, only 962 received a rule label. The detector agreed with 97.7% of those labels. The other 3,058 statements had no rule label to compare against. Agreement on the labelled subset measures how closely the detector reproduces the rules on the clearer cases. To estimate accuracy on the full set, an independent audit would need to sample the uncertain statements too. Rule coverage

Finding the Earlier Counterpart#

The search stage looks in the same party’s previous manifesto. BM25 rewards shared terms, while a dense MiniLM embedding ranks statements by vector similarity. Hybrid search combines the two rankings before a comparison model looks at specific pairs. I use top-five retrieval as a check on the search stage because a good counterpart that never enters the candidate list cannot be selected later.

The exploratory challenge had 11 queries, seven with an earlier reference match. The pledge filter admitted just five of those seven queries. Removing the filter let hybrid search recover six reference matches in the top five. The end-to-end system chose a correct match on two of the seven queries. These results illustrate a trade-off in this small challenge. A stricter filter can save comparison work and lose candidates before they reach search. Retrieval checks

The expanded evaluation adds a different retrieval check using exact repeats identified by normalised text. BM25, dense search, and hybrid search each recovered all 14 temporal exact matches in their top five before the filter. Each recovered 13 after filtering. The exact-repeat check is a test with known links and cannot estimate success on a paraphrase. Taken together, the two checks suggest inspecting filter coverage and candidate ranking separately before choosing a more elaborate reranker. Expanded retrieval and split results

Telling Repeated and Revised Promises Apart#

For a repeated commitment, the core obligation should survive a change in wording. For a revision, a material element changes, such as eligibility, a deadline, an amount, or what the party promises to do. A pair can also express separate commitments. The pipeline uses a MiniLM cross-encoder to read the two statements together and can abstain when its confidence is low. When it flags a revision, a separate set of rules proposes text spans for review.

The original training set combined historical pairs with controlled edits. Its overlap check compared statement IDs, normalised text, and similar wording with the evaluation material. It excluded 92 of 2,063 candidate pairs. The retained 1,971 included 467 revised examples, all constructed by editing quantities. This is an important detail when interpreting a result on changes in scope or obligation. The training examples expose the model to a specific type of revision. The screening checks also leave open the possibility of unrecognised paraphrases across splits. Comparison codebook · Split audit

On 35 scored reference pairs, the screened five-epoch model agreed with 30 decisions and reached 0.592 macro-F1. At the working cutoff of 0.65, it missed all three revisions. Twenty-five pairs were labelled different, seven same, and three revised. Lowering the cutoff to 0.50 in a retrospective check found one of the three. That check used the same test predictions, so the result needs confirmation on another set before changing the working cutoff. The reference decisions came from a language model. These counts describe agreement with those decisions, and the three revision examples are too few to establish natural revision recall. Fixed test results and threshold check

A Correct Label and a Useful Explanation#

On a flagged pair, a reader needs the proposed change as well as the two relevant passages. In the illustrative subsidy example, highlighting the word “subsidy” in both texts establishes a shared topic. Highlighting the phrase that limits eligible households tells the reader where the commitment changes. I treat label agreement and evidence quality as different checks. In the original fixed panels, no correctly labelled revision also had matching evidence spans on both sides. The archived review covered 16 of the 32 revised pairs flagged in 2024, so any evidence finding from that review applies to its covered subset. Evidence audit and comparison panels

On the same 250 controls, DeBERTa detected 62 revisions, three more than MiniLM, but predicted a revision for 138 pairs. MiniLM used the label 59 times, all on constructed revisions. The choice of model therefore affects both how many candidate changes a reviewer sees and how many turn out to be actual edits in this benchmark. Control metrics

The temporal panel made from original text had 275 pairs. Its 66 determinate weak labels covered only repeated or different commitments, with the remaining 209 marked uncertain. The results on constructed edits therefore help locate weaknesses in the model. To measure how well it finds natural revisions, the next evaluation needs independently labelled natural changes and supporting spans from both original documents. Expanded evaluation

Forecasting Topic Shares#

The forecast asks how the mix of topics among detected pledges may change. I grouped those pledges using ManifestoBERTa’s policy categories, then compared two forecasts for the 2024 election. One reuses the previous election’s shares. The other extends half of the recent trend. The comparison uses total variation distance, half the sum of the absolute differences between the predicted and observed topic shares. Lower values mean the two distributions are closer.

The carry-forward forecast had lower error for all five parties in the backtest. The tuned model also selected zero trend weight on the earlier data. The project exports exploratory 2029 estimates, whose usefulness depends on a short election history and on which statements the detector labels as pledges. The backtest target is the system’s estimated topic distribution for 2024. That target inherits the decisions made during pledge detection and topic assignment.

Here is the distance calculation on three illustrative categories. In the example, five percentage points shift from the middle category into the last one. Taking half the sum of absolute changes counts the transfer once.

python
from math import fsum

def total_variation(observed, forecast):
    differences = (abs(y - p) for y, p in zip(observed, forecast, strict=True))
    return 0.5 * fsum(differences)

observed = (0.50, 0.25, 0.25)
forecast = (0.50, 0.30, 0.20)
print(round(total_variation(observed, forecast), 3))  # 0.05

The vectors must list the same categories in the same order and each must sum to one. A policy area with no detected pledge still needs an entry of zero if the other election contains that category. This example illustrates the metric. The actual five-party backtest uses the categories and denominators documented in the repository.

What I Would Use the Pipeline For#

The strongest use is to narrow down the text a reader needs to inspect. Retrieval can surface earlier candidates and keep their PDF locations attached. The relation model and evidence spans can help prioritise pairs for review. Results on naturally revised pledges are still too limited for treating those labels as an inventory of policy changes. For forecasting, carrying forward topic shares is the baseline a more complex model would need to beat on additional election cycles.
