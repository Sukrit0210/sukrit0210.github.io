A film can divide an audience and still receive an excellent average rating from critics. If a regression model calls that film popular, which of those responses did it predict? My course project used exploratory analysis and linear modelling in R to examine movie attributes and ratings. Reconsidering the outcome makes the original question more precise.

Research Question#

How do the recorded attributes of a film relate to the rating it receives, and how does the interpretation change with the audience supplying that rating? The original project investigated patterns in movie popularity using data associated with IMDb and Rotten Tomatoes. This article develops a paired analysis that could distinguish predictable differences in ratings from differences introduced by changing the films included in the analysis.

A rating measures an assessment among people whose responses enter a platform’s calculation. Attendance, box-office receipts, and the number of people who have heard of a film are different outcomes. A small film can be rated highly by the few people who seek it out. A widely watched film can draw sharply mixed reviews. Calling both processes popularity obscures the unit of prediction and makes a fitted coefficient difficult to interpret.

Dataset and Unit of Analysis#

The useful starting unit is a film with a stable identifier, a set of attributes, and a rating whose source and scale are known. For a comparison between critics and audiences, I would keep separate columns for the two targets. For each I would record how it was constructed: a mean numeric score and a percentage of favourable reviews can share a numerical range while measuring different things. Converting a ten-point score to a hundred-point scale changes its units but cannot reconcile those definitions.

A paired design needs an explicit inclusion rule. If a film has an audience rating and lacks a critic rating, it can inform a model for audience scores. It cannot supply a within-film contrast. I would report the total number of films available for each outcome, the number with both, and the characteristics of films lost when requiring a pair. Missing critic coverage may concentrate among smaller releases. A model trained only on jointly reviewed films would then address a narrower population even if its code handled missing values correctly.

I would check names and release years before joining records, since remakes and shared titles can create incorrect pairs. Where available, the platform identifier or an audited crosswalk should determine the match. Distinct cuts or re-releases may deserve separate records if the ratings refer to different versions. An erroneous join can resemble a striking audience–critic disagreement and will survive perfectly conventional regression diagnostics until someone inspects the underlying film.

Exploratory Analysis before Fitting a Line#

My first plot would put each film’s audience score on one axis and its critic score on the other. If both scores measured comparable quantities in common units, a diagonal line would locate equal numerical assessments. Points above it would have a higher critic assessment, and points below it a higher audience assessment. If the scoring systems encode different quantities, I would use separate distributions and examine within-source ranks before interpreting any distance from that line.

The number of contributing reviews changes the interpretation of a point. A high audience rating based on a small number of votes may be more variable than one based on thousands. A vote-weighted analysis gives frequently rated films more influence and answers a different question from an analysis assigning equal weight to each film. I would begin with equal weights to describe films, then inspect a sensitivity analysis restricted to better-supported scores if counts are available. The two analyses would be labelled by the population they describe.

A useful check is to select examples by a rule fixed before reading their titles. For instance, take the largest positive and negative differences among films with both scores and sufficient coverage. Inspect the source pages or the underlying records for those examples. An incorrect match, an ambiguous score definition, or a missing-value code can explain an extreme point. A verified extreme remains a candidate for interpretation. Choosing only memorable films would favour a story already familiar to the analyst.

Suppose a film earns a high audience rating and a low critic rating. A predictor can be close to one outcome and far from the other. Calling both outcomes “popularity” hides that distinction.

Two Models with the Same Inputs#

The original course project used exploratory analysis, linear modelling, and prediction in R. For a follow-up comparison I would fit two regressions on the same training films using exactly the same candidate attributes. One would predict the audience outcome and the other the critic outcome. Holding the sample and predictors fixed gives differences between the fits a clearer interpretation. If the outcomes also have different units, each coefficient still needs to be interpreted on its own scale.

A reference model is essential here. Predicting the training mean for every held-out film measures how much accuracy is available without individual movie attributes. For a narrowly grouped collection, the mean can be a surprisingly competitive predictor. A model’s training fit may improve simply because it has enough terms to track peculiarities of that collection. The held-out comparison asks whether the information travels to different films.

A Reproducible Paired Evaluation#

This R example specifies the proposed analysis. It assumes a verified table called films with the example field names below. Runtime and release year illustrate predictors that can be defined before ratings are observed. The example is a design for a replication; the project summary does not supply coefficients, split-level predictions, or an error table from which to report empirical results.

r
fields <- c("film_id", "release_year", "runtime",
            "audience_score", "critic_score")
paired <- films[complete.cases(films[, fields]), fields]
stopifnot(!anyDuplicated(paired$film_id))

# Reserve whole later release years for evaluation.
years <- sort(unique(paired$release_year))
stopifnot(length(years) >= 3)
cutoff <- years[max(1L, floor(0.8 * length(years)))]
train <- subset(paired, release_year <= cutoff)
test <- subset(paired, release_year > cutoff)

evaluate <- function(target) {
  formula <- reformulate(c("runtime", "release_year"), response = target)
  fitted_model <- lm(formula, data = train)
  observed <- test[[target]]
  predicted <- predict(fitted_model, newdata = test)
  data.frame(film_id = test$film_id, target = target,
             observed = observed, predicted = predicted,
             error = observed - predicted,
             baseline_error = observed - mean(train[[target]]))
}
results <- rbind(evaluate("audience_score"), evaluate("critic_score"))

The split in this example reserves later release years together. It asks how a model trained on an earlier catalogue transfers to more recent releases. A random film-level split would instead ask about another film drawn from roughly the same mixture of years. Neither design is universally preferable. The choice should match the proposed use of the predictions. If a film appears in several records, assigning all of its records to one partition prevents it from serving as both training example and test case.

Prediction time matters as well. A model designed for use before release cannot use the final audience vote count as a predictor, even if that field is highly correlated with ratings in the complete database. A retrospective description may legitimately use variables collected later, provided the article makes the timing clear. I would divide candidate features into production information known before release and reception information measured afterwards, then report which set the model received.

Reading the Residuals#

For each target I would keep the signed error, the absolute error, and the corresponding error from the mean baseline. Signed errors reveal the direction of a miss. Absolute errors measure its magnitude. Two models with similar average absolute error can have very different weaknesses if one persistently overpredicts low-rated films and the other underpredicts highly rated films. A residual plot against the fitted value would reveal that difference more clearly than a single average.

Imagine two films receiving the same fitted score from the audience model. If the observed audience scores diverge, the observed attributes did not distinguish those outcomes sufficiently. Now suppose the corresponding critic predictions are accurate for both films. That pattern would identify an audience-specific discrepancy in these examples. It would not identify its cause. Selection into reviewing, shifts in genre preferences, and unrecorded differences in the films remain possible explanations.

I would inspect the largest discrepancies alongside a few cases drawn uniformly from the test set. The extreme cases help locate failures worth studying. The random cases help judge how representative those failures are. With two rating sources, the same film should remain visible in both panels of any comparison. Separate lists of the most surprising critic and audience scores make it difficult to tell whether one unusually difficult film is driving both lists.

Coefficients, Coverage, and Practical Interpretation#

A runtime coefficient in a linear model describes a change in the expected score with an additional minute, conditional on the other included attributes. Multiplying by ten gives a more readable contrast for films ten minutes apart. That contrast is useful only over runtime ranges with reasonable data coverage. Extrapolating a fitted line from typical feature-length films to an unusually short release would attach unjustified confidence to a thin part of the sample.

Even within a well-supported range, the coefficient is an association. Editing a film to make it ten minutes shorter changes the viewing experience and may change which scenes or storylines survive. The model does not contain a controlled experiment on alternate cuts of the same film. For that reason, a sentence about how ratings vary across observed runtimes is defensible, while a recommendation that shortening a movie will improve its reception needs a different design.

Scale limits also matter. A linear fit can return a value outside a platform’s allowable rating range, particularly for unusual combinations of attributes. I would report how often that happens and where. Clipping those values can improve a numerical score while concealing poor extrapolation. If bounded predictions are essential, the comparison should include an explicitly bounded model and evaluate it under the same held-out protocol.

A Sensitivity Analysis for Who Gets Reviewed#

Requiring both scores makes the comparison easier to interpret, but it can also change the films under study. I would start with all films eligible for the audience model and split them into those with and without a recorded critic score. Within each group I would tabulate release years, runtime, and any measured genre categories. A clear shift in those distributions would show that requiring a pair changes the observed catalogue. It would leave open whether the unmeasured characteristics change as well.

I would then fit the audience model twice. The first training set would contain all eligible audience-rated films from the training years. The second would contain only those training films with both ratings. Both fits would be scored on the same paired test films. This comparison holds the test outcome and test population fixed while changing training coverage. A separate analysis could evaluate both models on audience-rated test films without critic scores. That would reveal whether conclusions drawn from widely reviewed releases transfer to the less completely documented part of the catalogue.

An accompanying figure could give each release period one pair of points: the audience-model error among jointly reviewed films and the error among films lacking critic coverage. The caption should state the number of films behind each point. A period with ten observations deserves a different level of confidence from one with several hundred. I would retain the actual film identifiers in the underlying evaluation table so that a reported change could be traced to a different sample, a different prediction, or both. This extension makes selection into the dataset a visible part of the analysis.

Conclusion and Limitations#

The course project established a setting in which exploratory analysis and regression can connect film attributes with recorded audience and critic assessments. The strongest methodological conclusion is that a prediction of popularity is meaningful only after the outcome, its source, and its observation time have been specified. Those definitions determine which films enter the study, how the baseline is computed, and what counts as an error.

I would begin a replication by publishing a small data dictionary and a film-level ledger of the two held-out predictions. Readers could trace a reported average back to the films contributing to it. I would also show how the findings change when requiring a minimum amount of review coverage and when reserving later release years for validation. The portfolio documentation supports the original research question and approach. Numerical conclusions about either audience require the original fitted outputs or a newly run evaluation.
