A manifesto can describe a shortage of doctors and promise to open new clinics in the same paragraph. Counting words associated with shortages tells us something about the language it uses. A model that reads the whole paragraph might give more weight to the promise. Either score can look precise in a chart, even though the two methods are responding to different parts of the text.

I wanted to know how much that choice changes the conclusions I could draw from Indian election manifestos. In my earlier manifesto study, I kept sentiment as a simple word-count baseline alongside topic modelling. Here I expanded the corpus, applied a contextual classifier to the same passages, and checked which comparisons survived the change in method.

Building a Corpus across Four Elections#

The corpus contains 9,572 English paragraphs and list items from 21 Lok Sabha manifestos covering the 2009, 2014, 2019, and 2024 elections. It includes BJP, Congress, CPI(M), AIADMK, DMK, and TMC. BJP, Congress, and CPI(M) have an edition in all four years. DMK and TMC enter the corpus in 2014, while AIADMK is represented through 2019. Availability of a verified English edition shaped the sample. A missing party-year is a gap in coverage, so it cannot be treated as a year with zero discussion of an issue.

The extraction pipeline keeps a passage identifier, document identifier, PDF page, source URL, and the extraction method. It uses OCR where the PDF does not provide usable text. The page and URL serve as a way back to the source when a score looks surprising. The completed rerun reproduced the saved passage table exactly and matched both saved sentiment labels for every passage. That check addresses reproducibility of the pipeline. The accuracy of the extracted text and labels calls for separate checks. Source inventory · Edition notes

Why Keep the Unit Fixed?#

Choosing the passage fixes which words each method sees together. It also fixes what a percentage counts. A list of ten one-line commitments can contribute ten observations, whereas a single long paragraph that discusses the same proposals contributes one. Two party-year summaries can therefore differ partly because of how the documents are organised. Alongside the sentiment percentages I would keep passage counts and, for a follow-up audit, examine whether the conclusion holds under a second segmentation. The published results use the paragraph or list item as supplied by the original extraction pipeline.

Two Ways to Score the Same Passage#

The first method counts matches against 22 positive and 21 negative words from the pilot study. For positive and negative counts pp and nn, the score is (p−n)/(p+n+1)(p-n)/(p+n+1). Scores above 0.05 and below −0.05 receive positive and negative labels respectively. The middle interval is neutral. The second method uses pretrained CardiffNLP RoBERTa. For long passages it scores windows of up to 256 tokens, averages their class probabilities weighted by token count, and takes the highest average probability as the label. That gives both methods the same unit of comparison. RoBERTa was trained on English social media, so its use on policy documents introduces a domain shift. Scoring methods in the report

One Number, Two Different Denominators#

Agreement divides the number of identical label pairs by the number of passages scored by both methods. The negative share divides one method’s negative labels by its total number of scored passages. These two quantities can move independently. If a model changes some positive labels to neutral, agreement can fall while its negative share stays constant. And two models can assign the same overall number of negative labels to entirely different passages. The compact table above is enough to compare the totals. To locate the disagreements, start with the linked joint count table and then inspect examples with their passage IDs and source pages.

When a Party Comparison Changes#

A corpus-wide negative share blends discussion of many policy areas. To compare parties within an issue, I used ManifestoBERTa to assign one of 56 policy categories to shorter text units. The completed run produced 15,376 assignments across 9,493 passages. Three of four units assigned to a category give that category 0.75 passage weight. The 79 passages with no topic assignment still enter the overall comparison, and the sentiment label applies to the whole passage. “Positive” in the topic name Agriculture and Farmers: Positive is part of the policy code. The lexicon and RoBERTa supply the separate sentiment labels. Topic coverage

What Changes When I Look Within Agriculture?#

The BJP’s positive share under RoBERTa moved from 53.4% in 2019 to 51.4% in 2024 across the full manifestos. In the agriculture category it moved in the opposite direction, from 46.6% to 49.9%, with 50.6 and 54.9 weighted passages respectively. The full-document figures combine all the issues the party wrote about. The agriculture slice answers a narrower question with a different denominator. Party-year totals · Topic breakdown

Reversals and Their Size#

Among same-topic, same-election party pairs with at least 10 weighted passages per party, 145 of 415 comparisons reversed direction across methods (34.9%). For a reversal, the first method ranks Party A above Party B by negative share, and the second ranks Party B above Party A. This is a count of eligible pairwise comparisons. The pairs can share parties or documents, and the count is descriptive. For a particular reversal, I would inspect its gap size, number of weighted passages, and original pages before writing about the parties. A tiny reversal near a tie and a larger reversal with broad coverage deserve different readings. Party ordering results

The denominator of 415 includes 78 comparisons tied under at least one method. The remaining 192 comparisons preserved their ordering. Across the 145 reversals, the median difference between the methods’ estimates of the party gap was 8.2 percentage points, compared with 3.9 points for the comparisons that kept their ordering. For one concrete reversal in 2024 agriculture, Congress’s negative share was 7.8 points above TMC’s under the lexicon and 5.4 points below TMC’s under RoBERTa. These estimates used 31.9 and 54.5 weighted passages respectively. All comparisons · Gap sizes

Take an illustrative comparison in which the lexicon gives Parties A and B negative shares of 12% and 8% within the same topic. Suppose RoBERTa gives 14% and 18%. Both parties move upward when the method changes, yet their ordering reverses. The gaps are +4 and −4 percentage points when measured as A minus B. This example uses invented numbers to show why a comparison of overall negative shares cannot substitute for comparing the signed gaps within a topic.

python
def reverses_order(lexicon_a, lexicon_b, roberta_a, roberta_b):
    lexicon_gap = lexicon_a - lexicon_b
    roberta_gap = roberta_a - roberta_b
    return lexicon_gap * roberta_gap < 0

print(reverses_order(0.12, 0.08, 0.14, 0.18))  # True

This snippet illustrates the sign-change idea and assumes an eligible, fully observed pair. It excludes a tie from reversal and leaves topic weighting and minimum coverage to the analysis. Treat the project’s released tables as the source for the reported count. A further check can report the share of reversals beyond a pre-specified gap size so that nearly tied comparisons are visible alongside more substantial changes.

What the Sensitivity Checks Reveal#

Where the Methods Disagree on Original Text#

The overall disagreement rate was 35.2%. It was 48.3% among 811 passages flagged by a negation cue, 73.7% among 38 with a criticism cue, and 72.5% among 51 that mixed commitment and problem language. These groups may overlap, and the two smallest groups are useful for finding examples to read closely. For instance, the study traced a BJP 2009 passage about food security and eliminating hunger back to page 17. The lexicon labelled it negative and RoBERTa labelled it positive. The source lets a reader assess both the problem named and the promise being made. Disagreement counts · Source-linked examples

Negation Separates a Word Count from a Contextual Score#

The expanded audit compared 105 paired texts. Three were direct edits of source passages. The other 102 were constructed clauses added to manifesto passages. RoBERTa’s positive probability minus its negative probability moved in the expected direction for all 105 pairs, while the lexicon score stayed the same. The three-way RoBERTa label changed in 87 cases. The remaining 18 show how a score can move while the winning label stays put. The three direct source edits are too few to estimate performance on naturally occurring negation. Expanded negation results

Small Changes to Input Can Change a Label#

A swap of BJP and Congress names changed 9 of 296 RoBERTa labels and zero lexicon labels. Simulated OCR corruption at a 5% probability per eligible character changed 17 of 340 RoBERTa labels and 16 of 340 lexicon labels. These are controlled interventions on selected passages. The OCR results describe sensitivity to the simulation, while estimating errors in the source collection would require comparing extracted text with transcribed PDF pages. Similarly, the name swap measures sensitivity to this particular edit, without establishing what caused a model to change its decision.

A Third Model Can Introduce Another Choice#

The expanded audit scored the same 1,050 passages with two BART prompt formulations. Changing the neutral hypothesis altered 867 labels. The number assigned neutral rose from 20 to 887, and agreement with RoBERTa rose from 34.2% to 73.7%. That is a large change from wording alone. Without independent annotations, closer agreement with RoBERTa leaves open whether either output is closer to a reader’s judgment.

Reproducing the Comparison#

The repository separates extraction, scoring, topic assignment, aggregation, plotting, and the two sensitivity audits. That separation is useful because once the expensive scoring steps are complete, the aggregation can be rerun from the saved scores and topics. To inspect an unexpected comparison I would start with the party-year-topic output, identify contributing passages and their weights, then join back to the passage table by passage_id. Reviewing the original page is especially valuable when a passage contains a quotation, a list of several policy proposals, or text recovered by OCR.

The released run records the checkpoint versions and gives a command for rebuilding the data from source PDFs. Where the same source files are available, a useful replication report includes the document inventory, the number of passages exported per document, counts by extraction type, and the exact score table used for the charts. That provides a way to tell whether a changed conclusion came from revised models, a different set of PDFs, or an extraction difference. Model versions · Run instructions

What Can I Say About Tone?#

The corpus supports a claim about measurement sensitivity. The two methods produce different negative shares and can reverse the ordering of parties on an issue. To assess accuracy, I would sample passages across elections, topics, and extraction types, including both agreement and disagreement cases, and have independent readers annotate them against a common codebook. I would ask whether the tone describes a current condition, criticises another actor, or expresses a stance on a proposal. Those distinctions matter when a single passage includes more than one kind of language.

The passage table and its source links carry over to the companion pledge study. That project splits the passages into statements and asks whether a specific commitment recurs across elections. The move from describing tone to tracking commitments makes traceability to the original PDF even more important.
