Plot medical charges against BMI and two different slopes appear. Among smokers in this dataset, charges rise steeply with BMI. Among non-smokers the line is much flatter. I used that difference to examine a simple modelling decision: should both groups share one slope?

Research Question#

How well does an additive linear model predict annual medical charges, and how does its predictive error change when the model allows curvature and interactions? Smoking status and body mass index (BMI) provided a specific reason to examine the second question: the exploratory data suggested that the association between BMI and charges differed across smoking groups.

Dataset and Study Design#

The study used the teaching dataset distributed as insurance.csv, which the project report traces to Brett Lantz’s Machine Learning with R [1, 2]. The report describes its 1,338 records as simulated observations representing US insurance beneficiaries. Each record contains annual medical charges in dollars, age, sex, BMI, number of children, smoking status, and region.

I randomly split the records into 936 training observations and 402 test observations. I used the training data to fit the regressions and compared their predictions against charges in the test set. The outcome throughout was annual medical charges. Estimating an insurance premium would require additional information and assumptions.

What One Observation Represents#

An observation is a beneficiary’s annual charge. The same total could arise from many inexpensive visits or a single costly event. The file records the total alongside beneficiary characteristics. It does not break the total down into procedures or follow a beneficiary from one year to the next. That distinction limits the questions available to the analysis. A prediction for a profile can be compared with another annual charge, but the records alone cannot identify the clinical events responsible for an unusually large residual.

The distribution of charges is right-skewed. A relatively small number of expensive observations stretch the upper tail. Two consequences follow. First, the mean charge within a group can lie well above the amount paid by a typical member. Second, an error of several thousand dollars has a different practical meaning near the bottom and the top of the distribution. I therefore kept the dollar-scale outcome for interpretation and examined the distribution of residuals in addition to the average prediction errors.

The random split answers a specific question about another observation drawn from the same simulated population. A hospital receiving patients with a different case mix or an insurer operating in a later year poses a different prediction problem. A random split cannot measure the effect of those changes. This is especially important for synthetic teaching data, where a strong association can reflect a feature of the data-generating process.

Exploratory Analysis and Model Specification#

Following backward selection, the additive model retained age, BMI, number of children, and smoking status. Its estimated BMI coefficient was $319.50. Holding the other predictors fixed, this specification assigns the same increase in predicted charges to a one-unit increase in BMI in either smoking group. Smoking status can shift the fitted level, while the BMI slope is shared.

Figure 1 suggested a reason to relax that assumption. The expanded specification included a BMI-by-smoking interaction, allowing the BMI association to differ by group. It also included squared terms and an interaction between smoking status and number of children. These terms changed together, so the comparison evaluates the expanded specification as a whole.

How the Candidate Terms Were Selected#

I began with the available beneficiary characteristics and used backward elimination guided by AIC. At each step, removing a term reduces complexity but can also reduce fit. AIC places those competing effects on a common scale. In this dataset, smoking status, age, BMI, and number of children survived selection for the additive model. Removing a candidate term in this procedure says something about its incremental contribution given the other terms and this sample. It does not establish that the underlying characteristic is irrelevant in other populations.

The additive fit is worth retaining even when a more flexible formula performs better. Its constant BMI slope gives a concrete prediction that the scatterplot can challenge. A model that simply reports lower error provides less insight into which assumption was costing predictive accuracy. Here the grouped scatterplot points to a plausible limitation before looking at the test scores: a shift in intercept leaves the two predicted slopes parallel.

One interpretive detail matters in the selected expanded formula. It includes the square of age without a separate linear age term. The change in fitted charge between ages 30 and 31 therefore differs from the change between ages 60 and 61. Treating the coefficient of squared age as a dollar change for one extra birthday would give the wrong units. For such terms, I prefer reporting paired predictions at specific observed ages. That also avoids attaching practical meaning to the model’s intercept at a profile with zero age and zero BMI.

Fitting the Two Specifications in R#

The code below uses the report’s split and selected terms. The response is measured in dollars. Keeping that scale makes a prediction error directly interpretable as the amount by which an estimate missed the observed charge.

r
insurance <- read.csv("insurance.csv")
set.seed(100000000)
keep <- caTools::sample.split(insurance$charges, SplitRatio = 0.7)
train <- insurance[keep, ]
test <- insurance[!keep, ]

additive <- lm(charges ~ age + bmi + children + smoker, data = train)
expanded <- lm(
  charges ~ bmi + children + smoker +
    I(age^2) + I(bmi^2) + I(children^2) +
    bmi:smoker + children:smoker,
  data = train
)

The squared terms above express the raw second powers used in the report’s final model. At a fixed age and number of children, the BMI-squared term allows the fitted relationship to bend within each group. The BMI-by-smoking term changes its slope between groups. In the fitted model its coefficient was about 1,466: for a one-unit increase in BMI, the predicted charge difference between the two smoking groups widened by about $1,466, holding the other predictors fixed. This describes the fitted association in the simulated records.

Predictive Performance on Held-Out Records#

Test-set measure Additive model Expanded model
Mean absolute error $4,220.72 $2,876.25
Root mean squared error $6,109.69 $4,884.47

For record ii, let yiy_i be the observed charge and y^i\hat{y}_i the predicted charge. Taking the absolute value before averaging keeps underpredictions and overpredictions from cancelling. The resulting mean absolute error is in the same units as the charges.

MAE=1n∑i=1n∣yi−y^i∣\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|y_i-\hat{y}_i\right|

Both error measures can be calculated from the same vector of prediction residuals:

r
score <- function(model, held_out) {
  error <- predict(model, newdata = held_out) - held_out$charges
  c(MAE = mean(abs(error)), RMSE = sqrt(mean(error^2)))
}

round(rbind(
  additive = score(additive, test),
  expanded = score(expanded, test)
), 2)

Consider a $10,000 miss and a $1,000 miss. The first contributes ten times as much to the sum of absolute errors and one hundred times as much to the sum of squared errors. Reading both measures helps distinguish a reduction in typical dollar error from sensitivity to a few unusually costly cases.

The expanded model reduced mean absolute error by $1,344.47 per record, approximately 32% on this split. Root mean squared error also declined. This measure gives greater weight to large misses, so the improvement was evident under both criteria [2, printed p. 53].

The report also calculated error on a logarithmic scale. Its implementation excluded nonpositive predictions from the additive model’s logarithmic score. The expanded model produced no negative predictions and used the full test set for its score. The dollar-scale measures above provide the more direct comparison because both models were scored on identical records.

What the Residuals Reveal#

The fitted models can also be compared on the data used for estimation. Adjusted R-squared increased from 0.7534 to 0.8459. That result indicates a closer training fit after a penalty for additional terms. The reduction in held-out MAE supplies an independent piece of evidence: the improvement persisted when predicting the test partition. An increase in adjusted R-squared alone would have provided a weaker argument for using the expanded formula [2, printed pp. 43–44].

I next examined residuals against fitted charges. A residual is the observed charge minus its fitted value, so a large positive residual marks a profile whose cost was underestimated. The same average test error could arise from many moderate misses or from concentrated underprediction in a small, expensive subgroup. A residual plot retains information about the size, sign, and location of those misses that a single score discards.

r
plot(fitted(expanded), resid(expanded),
     xlab = "Fitted annual charge", ylab = "Observed minus fitted")
abline(h = 0, lty = 2)
lmtest::bptest(expanded)

The printed diagnostic results warrant a qualification to the original report’s concluding paragraph. The studentised Breusch–Pagan statistic fell from 93.21 for the additive model to 16.922 for the expanded model. The tests use different degrees of freedom, so the raw drop is insufficient to establish constant variance. For the expanded model the reported p-value is 0.03093, below the stated 0.05 threshold. The residual tests therefore still give evidence of unequal variance [2, printed pp. 51–52]. Better prediction and a remaining variance problem can coexist.

Why Individual Coefficients Need Care#

The report also evaluated variance inflation. For the additive model, the reported VIFs were close to one. In the expanded model they reached approximately 60 for BMI and its square. A raw variable and its square can move together strongly over the observed range. In that setting, several combinations of coefficient estimates can yield similar fitted values. A conclusion about the size of one coefficient is more sensitive to sampling variation than a comparison of predicted charges over a well-represented interval.

A useful follow-up would fit a hierarchical polynomial model with BMI centred at a representative training value, then compare predicted contrasts at BMI values with good coverage in both groups. Centring changes what the lower-order coefficients refer to. When the full corresponding terms are retained, equivalent parameterisations give the same fitted curve. One must take particular care with the selected age specification: centring a solitary squared term changes the model unless its implied linear component is accounted for.

An Evaluation I Would Run Next#

I would preserve the original test split as a record of the reported comparison, then examine sensitivity to the split using repeated resampling. Within each training partition I would repeat selection and compare three prespecified candidates: the additive model, the additive model with a BMI-by-smoking interaction, and the wider polynomial model. Evaluating the three on exactly the same held-out records would make each difference in error a paired comparison.

For interpretation I would retain a small table for each smoking group showing its sample size, mean signed error, MAE, and upper quantiles of absolute error. Mean signed error would reveal systematic overprediction or underprediction. MAE would describe the typical magnitude, while the upper quantiles would show the errors faced by the least accurately predicted records. A group with few observations would need its uncertainty stated explicitly. These are proposed checks. The project’s reported test errors are the aggregate results in Figure 2 and the accompanying table.

Conclusion and Limitations#

Allowing curvature and interactions improved held-out prediction in this simulated dataset. The remaining variation in residual spread matters if a reader needs a range of plausible costs for an individual. Predicting the mean charge and estimating the uncertainty around a new charge are distinct tasks. The analysis establishes the former comparison on the study split and leaves interval calibration for further evaluation.

The comparison used one train/test split and cannot isolate the contribution of the BMI-by-smoking interaction. A follow-up analysis could add that term alone to the additive model, compare both specifications across the same resampling splits, and report errors by smoking group and predicted charge range. Validation on an independent claims dataset would be necessary before extending the findings to an insured population.
