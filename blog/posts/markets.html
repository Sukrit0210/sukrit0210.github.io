A forecast can follow the shape of a stock-price chart remarkably well and still be of little use at the moment a prediction is needed. The distinction becomes clear if we cover the right side of the chart and ask what information was available on the last visible trading day.

Research Question#

Our study examined patterns in ten years of Indian stock-market records and explored four forecasting methods. The methodological question I examine here is how to compare those approaches fairly across firms. A claim about forecasting requires a dated prediction, a defined horizon, and a subsequent observation against which it can be scored. A fitted curve on the complete series answers a different question.

Data and Scope#

The collaborative project used official NSE records for 40 Indian companies over 2014–2024. I worked with Agneebha Ghosh, Siddhartha Biswas, and Sneha Barman. We examined groups of stocks and developed forecasts using ARIMA, Holt–Winters, support vector regression, and LSTM models. The portfolio summary records the scope and the methods. It does not contain the forecast ledger, the company-level errors, or a held-out ranking. The comparison design below is a worked extension that explains what evidence I would collect before ranking the methods.

For this extension I would define one row as a company on a trading date, identify the price field explicitly, and verify that the dates increase within each company. A row count alone gives little assurance about the series. Two identical dates may represent duplicate imports, while a gap may be an exchange holiday, a suspension, or a missing observation. A horizon of five rows should mean five trading observations under the stated calendar. That interpretation becomes ambiguous if missing dates are silently filled.

I would also record how corporate actions were handled. For instance, a split can introduce a large change in the quoted price without the corresponding change in economic value. If a model is fitted on adjusted prices and evaluated on unadjusted prices, its error combines the price convention with its predictive performance. For an historical backtest, the adjustment and its availability date must be documented. These are data checks I would report alongside any future benchmark; the existing project description does not document all of them.

What Could Each Model Have Known?#

Consider choosing a date and covering every observation to its right. A forecast made on that date can use the history to the left. Once the later observations are uncovered, we can check the forecast against them. This simple boundary matters as much for constructing predictors as it does for fitting the model.

Four Models, One Prediction Task#

ARIMA uses relationships among lagged observations and forecast errors, often after differencing the series. The order of differencing matters. Too little can leave a persistent trend in the residuals, while unnecessary differencing can amplify short-term noise. Model selection should take place within the historical window available for that forecast. Selecting an order after inspecting an entire decade gives the earlier forecasts information about later behaviour.

Holt–Winters updates estimates of level, trend, and seasonality. Its seasonal period has to correspond to an interpretable repetition in the data. A calendar week and five consecutive trading sessions do not always cover the same interval. I would first ask whether the training history supports a repeating pattern at the chosen period, then evaluate whether carrying that pattern forward improves held-out predictions. A convincing seasonal pattern in a fitted decomposition is a reason to investigate, not a test-set result.

Support vector regression requires converting the sequence into predictor–outcome pairs. One example is a vector of recent lagged changes paired with a price change over the next five sessions. For this construction the outcome occupies an interval extending into the future. At a given training cutoff, examples whose outcome interval extends beyond that cutoff must be excluded. Otherwise the final training rows can leak precisely the observations reserved for evaluation.

An LSTM receives ordered sequences and learns a representation across their time steps. This makes sequence length, target definition, scaling, and the amount of training data part of the comparison. Two overlapping windows can share nearly all their values. Randomly scattering such windows between training and validation can make validation look easier than forecasting a subsequent period. A chronological split applied before window construction makes the intended information boundary explicit.

The models therefore have different fitting procedures but can share a forecast contract. Each must deliver a prediction for the same company and target date, using information available at the same origin. Some methods generate several steps recursively, while others can be fitted directly to the chosen horizon. That difference belongs in the method description. It does not change the observation used to evaluate them.

Move the Origin, Keep the Horizon#

A forecast one step ahead and a forecast twelve steps ahead answer different questions. For a follow-up evaluation I would fix the horizon, make each model forecast from the same date, move the origin forward, and repeat. A last-observed-value forecast would provide a baseline at every origin.

For each company I would keep the errors paired by date. That makes it possible to ask whether a method improves on the baseline across many origins or whether a small number of unusual periods drive its apparent advantage.

A Worked Baseline#

To make the scoring rule concrete, suppose the last observed price is 100 and the price five trading sessions later is 104. A last-value forecast predicts 100 and makes an absolute error of 4. If a fitted model predicts 103, its error is 1. The model improves on the baseline by 3 price units at this origin. These are illustrative numbers. A single favourable origin cannot establish that a model routinely improves forecasts.

The following example constructs the baseline for one company from a cleaned, date-indexed series of closing prices. It returns the rows needed to evaluate a fitted model later. With a five-session horizon, the final five observations cannot be used as origins because their outcomes lie beyond the dataset.

python
import pandas as pd

def baseline_ledger(close, horizon=5, min_history=120):
    if horizon < 1 or min_history < 1:
        raise ValueError("Use positive horizon and history lengths")
    if close.index.has_duplicates or not close.index.is_monotonic_increasing:
        raise ValueError("Expected one observation per date, in order")
    if close.isna().any():
        raise ValueError("Resolve missing observations before evaluation")
    dates = pd.Series(close.index, index=close.index)
    ledger = pd.DataFrame({
        "target_date": dates.shift(-horizon),
        "baseline": close,
        "observed": close.shift(-horizon),
    })
    return ledger.iloc[min_history - 1:].dropna()

Here the negative shift constructs the outcome column for retrospective scoring. It would be a serious error to hand that column back to the prediction function as an input. I would store forecasts first and join outcomes afterwards using company, origin, target date, and horizon. This separate ledger also makes missing forecasts visible. If one method fails during a volatile period, silently dropping those dates only for that method could give it an artificial advantage.

Reading the Errors across Companies#

For each origin I would compute a paired difference: the fitted model’s absolute error minus the baseline’s absolute error. Negative values favour the fitted model. A mean difference answers how much error changed overall. The fraction of origins with a negative difference answers how often the model improved. A small number of large gains can drive the mean even if the model loses frequently. Reporting both quantities helps readers tell those situations apart.

Summary Unit of comparison Interpretation
Mean absolute error One company, one horizon Typical magnitude of a miss in that price series
Mean paired error difference Same origin for model and baseline Average change relative to carrying the last price forward
Fraction of improved origins Same company and target dates How often the fitted method wins the pair
Median company-level improvement One summary per eligible company How broadly gains extend across the panel

Pooling price errors from forty companies requires care. A ten-unit miss can have very different meanings at different price levels. A pooled average can be dominated by a high-priced stock or a company with many more observations. One approach is to report the dollar- or rupee-scale error within each company, then compare dimensionless skill across companies using a prespecified baseline. If the baseline’s average error is near zero, ratios become unstable. I would show the underlying errors and flag the series for which a ratio has little meaning.

The uncertainty of those summaries depends on the forecast schedule. With daily origins and a five-session horizon, adjacent forecasts share much of the same future interval. Treating those errors as independent would overstate the amount of information in the test set. I would inspect paired errors over time and use blocks of consecutive origins when estimating uncertainty. An alternative sensitivity analysis would score only non-overlapping origins. Both choices trade the number of observations against their dependence.

What to Plot before Declaring a Winner#

For one representative firm I would plot observed prices and forecasts on the same scale, with a vertical marker at the origin. Alongside that chart I would show the signed error through time. The first plot reveals scale and timing; the second makes systematic underprediction during rises or overprediction during declines easier to see. I would choose the example by a stated rule, such as the firm closest to the median improvement, so that the illustration can be interpreted in relation to the full panel.

For the panel, a sorted plot of company-level differences would be more informative than forty overlapping price series. Each company would contribute one mark, with a reference line at zero. A cluster near zero and a few extreme gains would support a narrower claim than a distribution consistently on the favourable side. A further breakdown by horizon would show whether an advantage lasts beyond the shortest prediction interval.

Two Checks before Interpreting a Forecast Advantage#

I would rerun the evaluation with two simple disturbances to the workflow. First, fit each model using data that physically ends at its forecast origin, then compare its output with the prediction produced by the full backtesting script for the same origin. If the values differ, some step may be learning from later observations. This check can catch a scaler fitted on the whole series, a centred moving average, or an interpolation that uses both sides of a gap. Each of those operations can be appropriate for describing a historical chart and inappropriate for a forecast issued on that date.

Second, I would compare forecasts on the common set of eligible cases and publish a separate coverage table. Suppose one method produces forecasts for every firm and another can run only after accumulating a long training window. A comparison across all available forecasts would give the two methods different dates. Restricting the comparison to their intersection restores fairness, but also changes the population to firms and dates where both could operate. Readers need both the paired score and the coverage fraction to decide whether the apparent advantage is relevant to their setting.

Finally, I would specify the unit of any conclusion. A smaller five-session price error answers a question about point prediction. A claim about forecasting direction would require a separately defined outcome and score. Nor does a numerical reduction in error establish what an implemented trading rule would have achieved. The project’s statistical question can be answered on its own terms by examining dated forecasts against subsequent prices, with enough detail for another reader to reconstruct the comparison.

Conclusion and Limitations#

The project documents the data scope, exploratory analysis, and models we developed. The rolling-origin experiment above is a proposed extension. A defensible ranking of these four methods would require the held-out errors, their horizons, and the common baseline.

The most important result of this methodological review is a specification of the evidence required for a model comparison. The original study provides a substantial set of companies and a range of forecasting approaches. It gives no basis here for attaching a numerical advantage to one of them. The proposed ledger would make such a claim auditable, because a reader could inspect which forecast was made when, which observation settled it, and how the baseline performed on exactly that case.

A useful first replication would choose a single target, a single horizon, and a small set of firms before extending to all forty. I would verify that a one-row change in the cutoff changes only the forecasts that could have used that row. I would check that training transformations are fitted anew within each window. Finally, I would establish the last-value benchmark and examine where it fails. This sequence makes discrepancies easier to locate than fitting every model first and trying to explain the final ranking afterwards.
